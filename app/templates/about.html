Polar Geographic Guide

Understand the science, classification standards, and geographic quadrants behind Antarctic iceberg tracking.

USNIC Naming Conventions

Antarctic icebergs are named and tracked by the **U.S. National Ice Center (USNIC)**. The names are structured logically based on the geographic quadrant where the iceberg was originally discovered, combined with a sequential number.

For instance, A23A denotes:

  • A: Originally calved in Quadrant A (Bellingshausen/Weddell Sea).
  • 23: The 23rd major iceberg calved and tracked in that quadrant.
  • A: A sequential suffix indicating that this is a sub-fragment resulting from a previous calving or split of the original iceberg A23.

Antarctic Tracking Quadrants

Quadrant A

Weddell / Bellingshausen Sea

Covers longitude spans from 0° to 90° W.

Includes major ice shelfs like Larsen Ice Shelf and Filchner-Ronne Ice Shelf. Famous icebergs calved here include the massive A23A.

Quadrant B

Amundsen / Eastern Ross Sea

Covers longitude spans from 90° W to 180°.

Home of the Pine Island Glacier and Thwaites Glacier (the "Doomsday Glacier"), key monitoring hotbeds for structural ice sheet integrity.

Quadrant C

Western Ross Sea / Wilkes Land

Covers longitude spans from 180° to 90° E.

Surrounds massive glaciers calved into the Ross Sea ice front. Historically generates highly stable, tabular flat icebergs.

Quadrant D

Amery / Eastern Weddell Sea

Covers longitude spans from 90° E to 0°.

Includes the massive Amery Ice Shelf and Fimbul Ice Shelf, draining key interior sheets of East Antarctica.

Scientific FAQ

An iceberg must meet a minimum structural threshold before the USNIC will assign a tracking identifier and log its coordinates. The size criteria is at least 10 Nautical Miles (NM) on its longest axis, or a total surface area of at least 20 Square Nautical Miles (sqNM).
The USNIC tracking databases and GIS portals are updated weekly on Fridays. The satellite feeds and RADARSAT observations are analyzed by polar specialists to determine precise center coordinates, lengths, and widths before generating standard CSV reports.
While iceberg calving is a natural cyclical discharge mechanism for polar glaciers, the accelerated rate of calving and structural fractures (such as the disintegration of major shelves like Larsen B/C) are strong indicators of atmospheric and ocean warming. Tracking area decay and structural speed serves as invaluable data for global sea-level rise models.
